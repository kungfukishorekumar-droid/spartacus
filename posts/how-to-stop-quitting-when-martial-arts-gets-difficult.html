How to Stop Quitting When Martial Arts Gets Difficult

Everyone meets the wall — month three, month eight, year two. Here is the anti-quitting toolkit that carries students past it.

Spartacus Martial ArtsBy Kishore Kumar31 May 2026 · 07:40 PMUpdated 13 Oct 20265 min read
How to Stop Quitting When Martial Arts Gets Difficult

Quick Answer

Quitting urges are predictable and survivable: shrink the goal to 'attend the next class', tell your coach you are struggling, revisit your progress evidence, and never decide during a bad week.

Every student hits a wall: the week training feels pointless, progress feels frozen, and quitting feels like relief. It happens at month three, at month eight, in year two. I hit walls on my own competitive journey. Getting past them took a toolkit, not just willpower.

Why the wall appears

  • The novelty fades — the excitement of starting wears off.
  • Progress slows — early gains are fast; later gains are smaller and harder to see.
  • Difficulty rises — techniques become more detailed and demanding.
  • Life gets busy — exams, work, family, illness.
  • Comparison — someone who started later seems to be progressing faster.

None of these mean martial arts is wrong for you. They are simply part of learning anything worthwhile.

Tool 1: Shrink the decision

Do not decide about martial arts. Decide about the next class. "Just attend Thursday" has carried more students through walls than any motivational speech. Read why attendance should be your first goal.

Tool 2: Tell your coach

Struggling silently is quitting in instalments. Say, "Coach, I'm in a slump." A good coach has specific remedies: a new challenge, a different role, a short-term goal, or simply acknowledgement that this phase is normal.

Tool 3: Consult the evidence

Watch a video from your first month. Read your old training journal. The wall lies about your progress; the record tells the truth. Read why to keep a training journal.

Tool 4: Never decide in a valley

Make a rule: any decision about quitting must be made after a good class, not during a bad week. Very few people want to quit straight after a good session.

Tool 5: Change the goal

If a belt feels far away, set a smaller goal: a cleaner kick, a longer stance hold, perfect attendance this month. Small wins restart momentum. Read the confidence formula.

Tool 6: Reconnect with why you started

Fitness, confidence, self-defence, stress relief, fun? Write it down. Often the reason still holds — it just got buried under the difficulty.

Is wanting to quit a sign it's not for me?

Usually not. Almost everyone who trains long-term has wanted to quit at some point. The urge is a normal response to difficulty, not a verdict on your suitability. Read why training feels hard in the beginning.

How do I help my child through the wall?

  • Listen first — ask what feels hard.
  • Talk to the coach together.
  • Agree on a fixed period before any decision.
  • Point out progress they cannot see.
  • Praise persistence, not just results.

Read how parents can support practice at home.

When is quitting the right call?

Sometimes it genuinely is: if the environment is unsafe or disrespectful, if there is an injury or health reason, if the schedule truly cannot work, or if, after a fair trial and honest effort, the activity simply does not suit the person. Changing to a different art can also be a good decision. The goal is to avoid quitting on a bad day, not to stay forever regardless of circumstances.
