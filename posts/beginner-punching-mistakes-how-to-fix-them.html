Beginner Punching Mistakes and How to Fix Them

Dropping the guard, punching with the arm alone, holding breath — the five most common beginner punching mistakes, fixed.

Beginner Punching Mistakes and How to Fix Them

Quick Answer

The five classic beginner mistakes: punching with arm only, dropping the other hand, locking the elbow, holding breath, and leaning in. Each has a simple fix — slow reps under coaching.

Every beginner makes these mistakes — I made all of them myself. Knowing them early saves months of unlearning later. Here are the most common beginner punching mistakes, why they matter, and simple fixes you can practise.

Mistake 1: Arm-only punches

Beginners punch with the shoulder and arm alone. The punch looks fast but has little power, and it tires the shoulder quickly.

Why it matters: real power starts at the floor. Legs drive, hips rotate, the torso turns, the shoulder extends — and the fist arrives last.

Fix: practise slow punches focusing only on turning the hip and pivoting the rear foot. Feel the punch being pushed by your body, not pulled by your arm.

Mistake 2: The dropping guard

As one hand punches, the other drifts down to the chest or waist.

Why it matters: in partner work or self-defence, a dropped hand leaves your face open.

Fix: touch your cheekbone with the resting hand on every repetition until it stays there automatically. In Karate, where the other hand pulls back to the hip, the same principle applies: the non-punching hand always has a job.

Mistake 3: Locking the elbow

Snapping the arm out to full lock, especially into the air, strains the elbow joint over time.

Fix: finish punches just short of full extension — crisp, never crunchy. Control the end of the movement.

Mistake 4: Bent wrist

A wrist that bends on impact is one of the most common causes of beginner injuries on bags and pads.

Fix: keep the back of the hand in line with the forearm, and land with the first two knuckles. Wear hand wraps for bag work, and start with lighter impacts. Read how to train safely as a beginner.

Mistake 5: Holding your breath

Beginners often punch in tense silence, then tire very quickly.

Fix: exhale sharply with every strike. Sound is allowed — a short "tss" or a kiai. Breathing out on impact also tightens the core. See breathing control for martial artists.

Mistake 6: Leaning into the punch

Reaching forward to hit a target that is too far away kills balance and exposes your head.

Fix: keep your head behind your front knee; let footwork close the distance instead. Read why footwork is the secret weapon.

Mistake 7: Telegraphing

Pulling the hand back, dropping the shoulder or tensing the face before punching tells your partner what is coming.

Fix: punch straight from the guard position. Practise in a mirror and watch for any "wind-up" before the punch.

A ten-minute practice routine

  1. 2 minutes of slow punches, focusing on hip rotation.
  2. 2 minutes of jab-cross in front of a mirror, checking the guard hand.
  3. 2 minutes of punches with a sharp exhale on each.
  4. 2 minutes of step-and-punch, keeping your head behind your knee.
  5. 2 minutes of relaxed shadowboxing combining everything.

How long to develop correct punching?

With regular coaching and some home practice, most beginners have a reasonably correct basic punch within a couple of months. Refining power, speed and timing continues for years.

Do these apply to Karate and Kickboxing?

Yes. The details of stance and the non-punching hand differ between arts, but power from the ground, a straight wrist, breathing and balance apply to Boxing, Kickboxing, Karate, Kungfu and Wushu alike. See boxing training in Chennai and Karate basics.
