Martial Arts Nutrition Basics for Young Students

No supplements, no fancy diets — just simple, sensible eating that supports a young martial artist's training and growth.

Spartacus Martial ArtsBy Kishore Kumar18 Mar 2024 · 06:35 PMUpdated 13 Oct 20265 min read
Martial Arts Nutrition Basics for Young Students

Quick Answer

Young students need normal, balanced home food: a light meal 1.5–2 hours before class, water throughout, and a proper meal after training. No supplements, no extreme diets — growing bodies need fuel, not restriction.

Parents sometimes ask me about protein powders for their ten-year-olds. Please put the shaker down. Here is what a young martial artist's nutrition actually requires — and it is mostly the good home food you already know.

This is general guidance. For children with medical conditions, allergies or specific dietary needs, please consult a paediatrician or qualified dietitian.

The basic principle

Growing children need enough energy for growth and activity. Restrictive diets, skipped meals and "weight cutting" have no place in children's training. The goal is regular, balanced meals, enough water and good sleep.

Before class

A light, familiar meal or snack about 1.5–2 hours before training: idli or dosa, a sandwich, upma, poha, fruit with curd, or a banana with a handful of nuts. Too close to class or too heavy, and training suffers — children may feel sluggish or get a stomach ache.

What if class is right after school?

Pack a small snack for the journey — a banana, a chapati roll, a few dates and nuts, or a homemade sandwich — and eat it at least 30–45 minutes before class if possible. Make sure your child drinks water after school too.

During class

Water. Just water, sipped at breaks. In Chennai's heat this is not optional — send a full bottle to every class. For very long or intense sessions in hot weather, the coach may advise more.

After class

A normal, balanced meal within a couple of hours: rice or chapati, dal or sambar, vegetables, and eggs, fish, chicken, paneer or curd according to your family's diet. Home food covers a child's recovery beautifully.

Does my child need extra protein?

Most children eating a varied diet get enough protein from food — dal, legumes, milk, curd, paneer, eggs, fish, meat, nuts and seeds. Include a protein source at each main meal. Protein supplements are generally unnecessary for children and should only be used on medical or dietitian advice.

A sample training day

  • Breakfast — idli with sambar, or oats with milk and fruit
  • School lunch — rice or chapati, dal, vegetable, curd
  • After school — fruit and a handful of nuts or a small sandwich
  • Training — water
  • Dinner — chapati or rice, dal or sambar, vegetables, egg, fish, chicken or paneer

What to limit, gently

  • Sugary drinks and packaged juices marketed as "energy"
  • Deep-fried snacks right before class
  • Skipped breakfasts on training days
  • Very large meals just before training

No need to ban treats — balance matters more than perfection.

Are energy drinks okay for teens?

Caffeinated energy drinks are not recommended for children and teens. They can affect sleep, heart rate and anxiety. Water, milk, buttermilk and tender coconut water are better choices.

Hydration tips for Chennai

  • Drink water throughout the day, not just at class.
  • Pale yellow urine is a good sign of hydration.
  • Watch for headaches, dizziness or unusual tiredness — signs of dehydration.
  • On very hot days, buttermilk or tender coconut water after class can help.

Sleep is part of nutrition

Food and sleep work together. A well-fed child who sleeps poorly will still struggle to recover. Read why rest and recovery matter.

No supplements. Really.
