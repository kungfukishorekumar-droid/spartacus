How Martial Arts Builds Respect at Home and School

Respect cannot be lectured into children — but it can be trained. Here is how martial arts makes respect a physical habit.

Spartacus Martial ArtsBy Kishore Kumar27 Apr 2020 · 09:35 AMUpdated 13 Oct 20265 min read
How Martial Arts Builds Respect at Home and School

Quick Answer

Martial arts turns respect into a repeated physical practice — bowing, listening, thanking partners — until it becomes reflex. That reflex then appears at home and school naturally.

Every parent wants a respectful child. Very few children become respectful through lectures. In the academy, we do not lecture respect — we rehearse it, hundreds of times, until it becomes the way a child naturally behaves.

Respect as a physical habit

Students bow when entering the mat, greet the coach, thank their partners, and wait their turn. Repeated weekly, these actions stop being rules and start being reflexes. The same goes for smaller habits: lining up shoes neatly, caring for shared equipment, listening without interrupting.

Why the body teaches the mind

Children learn through movement and repetition far better than through words. A bow performed a thousand times carves a groove that "be respectful, okay?" never will. Over time the behaviour comes first and the understanding follows — the child begins to feel respect, not just perform it.

The kinds of respect martial arts trains

  • Respect for teachers — listening, following instructions, responding clearly.
  • Respect for partners — controlling techniques, thanking them, helping them improve.
  • Respect for beginners — senior students are expected to help newer ones, never mock them.
  • Respect for the space — keeping the training area clean, caring for equipment.
  • Self-respect — keeping promises to yourself, training honestly, standing tall.

Is bowing religious?

No. In martial arts, the bow is a gesture of respect and readiness — similar to a handshake or folded hands in greeting. It marks the start and end of training and shows respect to a partner. Read the real meaning of the martial arts bow.

The transfer home

Parents commonly notice the changes in small ways first: waiting for others to finish speaking, saying thanks without prompting, handling disagreements more calmly, answering when called. These are not dramatic, but they add up to a calmer home. Read the changes parents notice at home.

How parents can strengthen it

  1. Use the same language — "Ready position" for calming down, "Yes, coach" becomes "Yes, Amma".
  2. Model it — children copy what they see. Thank them, listen to them, apologise when you are wrong.
  3. Praise specific acts — "You waited for your sister to finish — that was respectful."
  4. Talk to the coach — if there is a specific behaviour at home, a good coach can reinforce the value in class.

For more ideas, see making martial arts a family value.

My child is rude at home. Will this really help?

It often does, but it is not instant and it works best together with consistent parenting. Martial arts provides a structured environment where respectful behaviour is expected, practised and rewarded every week. Over time, many children carry that behaviour home — especially when parents notice and praise it.

What if my child misbehaves in class?

A good coach handles it calmly: a clear reminder, a short pause from the activity if needed, and a chance to return and get it right. Never humiliation, never fear. The aim is to teach self-control, not to punish. Read what martial arts teaches that schools cannot always teach.

The coach's role

Children copy what respect looks like from their coach. That is why we model it — respect flows down before it flows out. A coach who shouts at children cannot teach them respect; a coach who is firm, fair and kind can. Come watch a class and see the culture yourself: book a free trial class.
