Kungfu Lesson: Softness Can Defeat Tension

The tense fighter is slow, tired and readable. The old Kungfu principle of softness is not weakness — it is efficiency wearing silk.

Spartacus Martial ArtsBy Kishore Kumar8 Aug 2025 · 07:45 AMUpdated 13 Oct 20264 min read
Kungfu Lesson: Softness Can Defeat Tension

Quick Answer

Softness in Kungfu means moving without wasted tension — relaxed muscles are faster, last longer and hide intentions. Tension telegraphs, exhausts and slows; softness conserves and strikes.

Try this. Clench your fist as hard as you can and punch slowly through the air. Now relax your arm, let it travel quickly, and tighten your fist only at the very end. Feel the difference? You have just touched a lesson Kungfu has refined over centuries.

Tension is expensive

A tense body pays a price in every muscle, every second. Tension burns stamina, slows movement, and broadcasts your intentions before you act. Beginners are often exhausted within minutes — largely from unnecessary tension, not effort.

Tension slows you down

When opposing muscles tighten at the same time, they work against each other, like driving with the brakes on. A relaxed arm can accelerate freely. That is why the fastest strikers look loose until the moment of impact.

Softness is loaded relaxation

Softness is not floppiness. It is a body relaxed and ready, spending tension only at the moment it is needed — like a whip: soft along its length, sharp at the tip. Strong structure (good stance, aligned joints) combined with relaxed muscles is the goal.

Softness in defence

A tense body is easy to push, pull and unbalance. A relaxed, rooted body can absorb, redirect and yield, then respond. Many traditional Chinese arts teach redirecting force rather than meeting it head-on — an idea Judo practitioners will recognise too.

Training the release

  • Slow forms — hunting for hidden tension in the shoulders, jaw, neck and fists.
  • Low, unhurried breathing, even during effort. See breathing control.
  • Contrast drills — tense, then release, until release becomes the default.
  • Relaxed shadowboxing — strikes thrown loosely, tightening only at the end.
  • Partner sensitivity drills — feeling a partner's force instead of resisting it.

How do I find my hidden tension?

Check common spots during training: shoulders rising towards the ears, a clenched jaw, fists squeezed between techniques, held breath, a furrowed brow. Ask your coach to watch for it, or film yourself. Slow practice reveals tension that fast practice hides. Read why slow practice builds fast reactions.

Isn't relaxed fighting weak fighting?

No. Relaxation is what allows real power. A relaxed strike is faster, and speed contributes greatly to impact. Tension at the moment of contact provides solidity. Strong martial artists combine both: relaxed in motion, solid on impact.

Is this related to Tai Chi ideas?

Yes. Tai Chi (Taijiquan) is famous for its emphasis on softness, relaxation and yielding, practised slowly. The same principle appears across Chinese martial arts and in many other combat sports — boxers talk about "staying loose" for the same reasons.

The life lesson inside

Gripping problems harder rarely solves them — at work, in families, in Friday evening traffic. A relaxed mind, like a relaxed fist, responds faster and better. Read calm face, strong heart and why martial arts is a lifestyle.
